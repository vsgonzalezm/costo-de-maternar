## PART 2 — SECTION OUTLINES

### a. Executive Summary (1 page) — outline

1. **Hook / problem (2–3 sentences).** Chile debates discrimination intensely — migration wave, gender gaps, class and ethnoracial hierarchies, Ley 20.609 — yet virtually all national evidence is perceptual (surveys, complaints). No systematic *causal* evidence exists on how much discrimination actually occurs, against whom, and under what conditions.
2. **The project (1 short paragraph).** Create the first experimental infrastructure in Chile producing causal evidence on discriminatory *behavior*. 
3. **The 18-month deliverables (concrete).** (a) Validated Chilean signal bank (names, photos, CVs signaling class, migrant origin, ethnicity, gender) — a methodological public good; (b) a flagship study: a labor-market correspondence study; (c) open protocol + data repository; (d) submission of at least one WoS paper and one large external grant application.
4. **High risk / high reward framing.** The open scientific problem: no validated way to signal race/class/migrant status in the Chilean context — genuine methodological frontier, exportable to Latin America and the Global South.
5. **International collaboration.** Established co-authors Weisshaar (Northwestern) & Chavez (Indiana) as founding partners with formal commitment and in-kind co-financing (advisory time, comparative US data); ASR 2024 track record.
6. **Seed logic.** Explicit runway: Avanza → Fondecyt Regular 2028 + international bid (see Part 4), positioning the lab as a Núcleo Milenio–scale candidate.


Problem: discrimination in Chile (broadly and in the labor market)

In the 2020 Latinobarómetro (CITE), 35.3% of Chilean respondents (n=1,174) described themselves as part of a discriminated group — the second-highest self-report rate among the 18 countries surveyed, behind only Brazil (40.3%), and well above both the South America regional average (25.4%) and the broader Latin American pattern seen in Mexico & Central America (16.9%) and the Caribbean (14.9%). On the perception measure, Chileans' top-ranked discriminated groups were poor people (24.0%), indigenous and native people (18.1%), older adults (14.6%), immigrants in general (9.8%), and homosexuals (6.7%). While "poor people" was also the most-cited group regionally (South America average: 27.7%, essentially matching Chile), Chile diverged from its region in the salience of the other categories: indigenous people were named substantially more often than the South American average (18.1% vs. 11.0%), as were older adults (14.6% vs. 5.3%) and immigrants (9.8% vs. 3.2%). Together, these results suggest that Chileans not only report experiencing discrimination at a notably higher rate than their regional peers, but also perceive a broader and more intense pattern of group-based discrimination in their society — particularly around ethnicity, age, and immigration status — than is typical elsewhere in South America.

ENCLA: in 9.1% of firms with five or more employees, workers or union representatives reported witnessing or knowing of at least one form of discriminatory action in 2022. This prevalence scaled sharply with firm size — from 4.5% in small firms to 18.1% in medium-sized firms and 25.4% in large firms. Discrimination by sex/sexual orientation/ethnicity/skin color/language/nationality or social origin, was reported in 2.3% of firms overall: 12.6% in large firms, 5.4% in medium firms, and 1.7% in small firms (Dirección del Trabajo, 2025, ch. 5). It is key to note that self-reports of discrimination can understate the true prevalence of discrimination. 

Contribution of the signal bank:
Chile's near-total absence of audit studies — only two ever conducted, one in labor markets (Bravo, Sanhueza, and Urzúa 2008) and one in consumer credit (Montoya et al. 2025) — is symptomatic of a broader pattern across Latin America, where experimental approaches to discrimination remain a nascent methodology. Figure XX shows the prevalence of experimental methods used in Web of Science papers in the region, and how it compares to production in the United States. This scarcity does notstudying  mean there is a lack of interest in discrimination or biases, but it reflects a missing methodological infrastructure. In the United States, the maturation of correspondence audit studies (as well as other experimental methods in social sciences) has been enabled by a dedicated line of validation research. For example, there has been systematic retesting of how names signal race, ethnicity, and class before they are deployed as experimental treatments (Gaddis, 2017a; 2017b). No equivalent validation work exists for Chile or, to my knowledge, for Latin America more broadly: researchers wanting to signal gender, social class, migrant, or indigenous status, through names or other cues have had to rely on ad hoc classifications (as Bravo et al. 2008 did, using informally sorted "upper-class" and "lower-class" surnames from a single university alumni list) rather than a validated, published instrument. The signal bank proposed in Phase 1 — pretested and validated ratings of perceived class, origin, and ethnicity for a systematic set of Chilean names and other cues, released as a citable, reusable resource — is therefore a contribution to further developing this field in the region. By lowering the fixed cost of building credible experimental treatments, it is designed to move the field from isolated, one-off audits toward a cumulative, methodologically rigorous research program, and make experimental designs a more accessible and trusted tool for studying discrimination in Chile and across the region.

- **H1 (behavior):** Discriminatory treatment in the Chilean labor market varies systematically by migrant origin, class signals, and gender.
- **Methodological conjecture:** Class, gender, and ethnoracial categories in Chile can be validly signaled through locally calibrated cues (names, communes, schools, phenotype), enabling a replicable Latin American audit and survey experiment paradigm.

For employment focus:
There is a lack of knowledge on whether and to what extent there is discrimination in hiring processes in Chile. In terms of perceptions or reports of discrimination during the hiring process, Chile's most comprehensive employer survey, the ENCLA (Encuesta Laboral), does not cover recruitment or hiring processes at all (Dirección del Trabajo, 2025). There is some qualitative evidence showing self-report experiences of discrimination in hiring, particularly in terms of gender and motherhood (CITE Undurraga).
Regarding experimental data that aims to measure actual discriminatory behavior, there is only one study to date, conducted more than a decade ago (CITE Bravo), when the labor market conditions and characteristics where drastically different from today.
